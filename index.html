Historical context

The Tesla Connection: What's Real and What's Marketing?

Energy Revolution System leans on Nikola Tesla's name, so it's worth knowing the actual history.

U.S. Patent 512,340, "Coil for Electro-Magnets," was granted to Tesla on January 9, 1894. It describes a bifilar coil — two wires wound side by side and connected in series — which increases the coil's self-capacitance. Tesla's goal was to improve how coils behave in alternating-current circuits.

What the patent does show: Tesla was a serious, inventive engineer and bifilar coils are a real, useful design still used today.

What it does not show: that a coil can produce more energy than it receives, run a home for free, or replace solar panels. Tesla's patent never claimed that.

Bottom line: "Tesla-inspired" is accurate as history and inspiration. It is not proof of performance.

Technical reality check

The Physics: Energy Conservation and Power Factor

1. Energy can't be created from nothing

The first law of thermodynamics says energy in = energy out + losses. Every generator, from a hydro dam to a bicycle dynamo, converts energy from somewhere else. If a device appears to make "extra" power, the measurement is wrong or an outside source is hidden. Ask one question of any DIY energy design: where does the energy come from?

2. Power-factor devices don't shrink home bills

Some "energy saver" gadgets claim to cut bills by correcting power factor. Homes in the U.S. are billed for real energy (kWh), not apparent power, so improving power factor at the outlet doesn't reduce what you pay. The U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) has explained that residential power-factor correction devices should not be expected to lower electric bills.

3. What does work

Proven savings come from using less energy (efficiency), shifting use to cheaper hours, or generating from a real external source like sunlight. Those are the lenses we use throughout this guide.

Savings question

Can Energy Revolution System Reduce Your Electric Bill?

Possibly a little, if you follow the efficiency parts and measure honestly. Not by 70–88% from a homemade coil device.

The parts of the program that focus on understanding usage and cutting waste can lead to real savings — the same savings any good energy audit would find. A DIY generation device will only lower your bill if it delivers measurable kWh from a genuine outside source and is connected legally and safely, which usually means a licensed electrician and, for grid-tied systems, utility approval.

The good news: you can test it yourself. If your metered usage doesn't drop within the 60-day window, you can request a refund.

Measurement checklist

  • Baseline: 30+ days of kWh use before any change (weather-adjusted if possible).
  • Input: energy going into the device, in watt-hours.
  • Output: usable energy delivered, measured with a calibrated meter.
  • Losses: heat, wiring and conversion losses.
  • After: 30+ days of kWh use with the change in place.
  • Compare: same season, similar weather, same household routine.
Preparedness

Can It Provide Backup Power During a Blackout?

Backup power is a system, not a single gadget. Any setup — DIY or commercial — needs all three of these pieces to keep lights or a fridge running safely.

☀️

1. A real energy source

Solar panels, a wind turbine, a fuel generator or a pre-charged battery. Without stored or captured energy, nothing runs when the grid is down.

🔋

2. Storage & conversion

A battery sized for your loads, plus an inverter and charge controller, with fuses and breakers to protect against overload.

🔌

3. Safe isolation

A transfer switch or interlock installed by an electrician — so you never back-feed the grid and endanger utility line workers.

Never back-feed your home by plugging a power source into a wall outlet ("suicide cord"). It can electrocute line workers and start fires.

Safety first

Electrical Safety: Read This Before Any DIY Energy Build

Homemade electrical projects cause fires, shocks and insurance problems every year. Follow these rules no matter which guide you use.

⚠️

Stay low-voltage

Experiment with small batteries and low-voltage DC. Mains voltage (110–240V AC) can kill.

🚫

Never touch the panel

Don't connect DIY devices to your breaker panel or outlets. That's a job for a licensed electrician.

🔥

Fuse everything

Use correctly rated fuses, wire gauge and enclosures. Batteries can deliver huge currents instantly.

🧯

Plan for fire

Build on non-flammable surfaces, keep a Class C extinguisher nearby, and never leave tests unattended.

📋

Check codes & insurance

Permanent installs may need permits and inspection. Unpermitted work can void home insurance.

🔋

Respect batteries

Lithium cells need proper chargers and a battery management system. Damaged cells can ignite.

Plain-English terms

Home Energy Glossary

kWh (kilowatt-hour)
The unit on your bill. Running a 1,000-watt device for one hour uses 1 kWh.
Watt (W)
The rate of energy use or production at a given moment.
Bifilar coil
A coil wound with two parallel wires, as in Tesla's 1894 patent.
Power factor
How efficiently current is turned into real work. Homes are billed for real energy, so it rarely affects residential bills.
Inverter
Converts battery DC power into household-style AC power.
Transfer switch
Safely disconnects your home from the grid before backup power is connected.
Back-feeding
Sending power into the grid through home wiring — dangerous and often illegal.
